LEHUA: Good. First question. "How many times should I take the ACT?"
DEREK: Usually two. Sometimes three. Taking it twice is smart for most people. The first time, you learn what the real day feels like. The second time, you're calmer, and you've had time to fix your weak spots. Three is fine if your score is still climbing. After that, if your score isn't moving, more test dates won't fix it. Your practice needs to change, not your number of attempts.
PONO: And leave real study time between them. If nothing changes in your practice between two test dates, don't expect much to change in the score.
LEHUA: Second question, and it's related. "What's a superscore, and does it help me?"
DEREK: Superscore means ACT takes your best English, your best Math, and your best Reading from different test dates and averages them into one Composite. Let's do it, because this one's easier to hear with numbers.
DEREK: First test. English twenty-four, Math twenty-six, Reading twenty-two. Second test. English twenty-seven, Math twenty-three, Reading twenty-five. What's the superscore Composite?
DEREK: One more time. First test, twenty-four, twenty-six, twenty-two. Second test, twenty-seven, twenty-three, twenty-five. Take the best of each and average them.
LEHUA: Pause it and try.

KADEN: Best English is twenty-seven, from the second test. Best Math is twenty-six, from the first. Best Reading is twenty-five, from the second. Twenty-seven plus twenty-six plus twenty-five is seventy-eight. Divided by three is twenty-six.
DEREK: Twenty-six. And here's why it matters. The first test alone averages to twenty-four. The second alone is twenty-five. The superscore is higher than either one. If you picked twenty-five, you used the latest test instead of the best of each. The app calls that misread the question.
KADEN: So I don't have to have my best day on every section at once.
DEREK: Right. And you don't have to ask for it. Once you've tested more than once, the superscore shows up in your MyACT account on its own. Many colleges accept the superscore. Some only look at your best single test date. Check each school on your list.
LEHUA: Next question. "I can't afford the test. What do I do?"
SARAH: Go to your counselor. Say it just like that. There are fee waivers for juniors and seniors who qualify. A waiver covers up to two tests, and it covers sending your scores to up to six colleges, with extra reports free after that. The test runs about seventy dollars this year for English, Math, and Reading, seventy-five with Science, ninety-five with Writing. Check act dot org for the current price.
SARAH: And everything in this app is free, and the official practice test is free. You do not need to buy anything to get ready.
LEHUA: Next. "A lot of schools are test-optional now. Why take it at all?"
SARAH: Test-optional means you choose whether to send a score. It doesn't mean scores don't matter. UH's flagship campus is test-optional, for example. If your score is good for a school, sending it can help your application. If it's not, you don't send it. But you can only make that choice if you have a score.
PONO: And some schools do look at scores. UH Hilo considers ACT or SAT scores for first-year applicants. Both tests, no favorites.
DEREK: For context, the national average Composite for the class of twenty twenty-five was nineteen point four. That's from ACT's own report. But whether your score is good depends on where you're applying, not on the national average.
LEHUA: Next one is big. "Should I take the Science section?"
SARAH: Same answer as episode one. Take it if you're thinking about engineering, nursing, pre-med, or any science major, or if a school on your list asks for it. When in doubt, take it. A school that doesn't use it can ignore it. Science gets its own score, and it's not part of your Composite.
DEREK: And if you take it, you get a STEM score too, the average of your Math and Science.
KADEN: And it's mostly graphs. Not biology.
DEREK: Mostly graphs. Episode eleven.
LEHUA: Then turn on the Science toggle in Settings, so the app gives you Science drills. Next. "Should I take both the SAT and the ACT?"
DEREK: Take one practice test of each, timed. That's episode two. Then pick one and go deep. Colleges that look at scores take either one. Splitting your prep between two formats usually slows you down on both. If both practice tests felt about the same, pick the one whose pace felt better.
LEHUA: Okay, this one came in a lot. "I get really nervous on tests. What helps?"
PONO: This one's mine. Okay, real talk first.
PONO: Nerves are normal. Everybody in that room is nervous. The best thing for nerves is making test day feel familiar. Take your practice tests timed, at the same hour as the real one, with the same pencils or the same screen. When the real day comes, it's just one more practice test that counts.
SARAH: And have a plan for the first question. A lot of people blank on question one. So decide now. If question one looks weird, you mark your best guess, flag it, and go to question two. You don't let one question set the tone.
PONO: And if your worry about tests feels bigger than normal nerves, talk to your counselor. Same if you think you might need accommodations. Talk to your counselor about accommodations.
LEHUA: Next. "I work after school. How do I study with a job?"
DEREK: Short sessions, every day, beat one long session on the weekend. When you set up the app, you told it how many minutes a day you have. The weekly plan on the dashboard uses that number. A drill is ten questions, about fifteen minutes. Blitz is sixty seconds. You can do a Blitz on your break.
KADEN: I do Blitz on the bus. It's the only time the bus is useful.
SARAH: And the error log keeps you honest. If you only have twenty minutes, do whatever's due in the error log first. That's the highest-value twenty minutes you can spend.
PONO: And protect your sleep, especially the week of the test. Studying late every night is not a plan.
