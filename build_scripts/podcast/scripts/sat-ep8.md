SARAH: Love it. And today is still about how sentences connect. Last time it was the punctuation between them. Today it's the words.
LEHUA: Transitions. Sarah, go.
SARAH: Transition questions give you two sentences with a blank at the start of the second one, and four words like however, therefore, for example, in addition. Here's the move. Before you look at the choices, name the relationship between the sentences. There are really only five. Add, which means more of the same. Contrast, which means it goes against what came before. Cause, which means the second one is a result of the first. Example, which means the second one shows the first. And sequence, which means it comes next in time.
KADEN: Why can't I just try each word and see which one sounds smooth?
SARAH: Because they all sound smooth. That's the whole trick. Transition words are built to flow. "Therefore" sounds great in almost any sentence. The question isn't which word sounds good. It's which word means the right relationship.
DEREK: It's like a plus or minus sign. The numbers don't change, but the sign changes the whole answer.
LEHUA: Okay, let's try one. Sarah, read it twice.
SARAH: Here it is. "The team practiced every morning for a month. Blank, they lost their first game by twenty points."
SARAH: Again. "The team practiced every morning for a month. Blank, they lost their first game by twenty points."
SARAH: Before the choices, name the relationship. Then here they are. A, therefore. B, nevertheless. C, for example. D, in addition.
SARAH: One more time. A, therefore. B, nevertheless. C, for example. D, in addition.
LEHUA: Pause it and try. Name the relationship first.
LEHUA: And we're back. Kaden, what's the relationship?
KADEN: Honestly, I almost said therefore. They practiced, therefore, something happened.
SARAH: That's the one the test hopes you pick. What would you expect after a month of practice every morning?
KADEN: That they'd play well. That they'd win, or at least not get destroyed.
SARAH: And what actually happened?
KADEN: They lost by twenty. Oh. So it went against what you'd expect. That's contrast.
SARAH: Contrast. Now cut every choice that isn't contrast. "Therefore" is cause. It says the loss happened because of the practice, which flips the meaning. In the app, that trap is called opposite. It's the right topic, pointing the wrong direction. "For example" is example, and the loss isn't an example of practicing. "In addition" is add. So the only contrast word left is B, nevertheless. Nevertheless means "even so," or "in spite of that." In spite of all the practice, they lost. The answer is B.
KADEN: So "therefore" was basically the opposite of the right answer.
SARAH: Exactly. Cause and contrast pull in opposite directions. One says, "and so, as expected." The other says, "but, against what you'd expect." When you see two choices from opposite categories, one of them is usually the trap.
LEHUA: What if two choices are in the same category? Like however and nevertheless?
SARAH: Then the test won't make you choose between them in the same question, or one of them will fit the exact shade better. That's step four. Pick the word that fits the exact meaning. But most of the time, once you cut the wrong categories, you're down to one.
KADEN: It's like a combo in a fighting game. The next move depends on what you just did. You can't just mash buttons.
SARAH: That's a good one. Don't button-mash transitions.
LEHUA: Now the notes questions. Those look scary.
SARAH: They look long, and they're actually fast. You get a list of bullet-point notes a student took, and the question says, "The student wants to do something. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?" The move is, read the goal first. Before any of the notes.
DEREK: Read the question first. That's been the move for four episodes now.
SARAH: Because it keeps working. Say the goal is, "emphasize a similarity between two programs." Every choice will use real facts from the notes. Every single one is accurate. The right one is the one that does the goal. If a choice explains how one program started, it's true, it's from the notes, and it doesn't show a similarity. Cut it.
KADEN: So you don't even have to read all the notes.
SARAH: You can skim them. The answer is decided by the goal. Circle the key words of the goal, like "similarity" or "contrast" or "introduce the study to a new audience," and find the choice that does exactly that.
