LEHUA: Good. Today is the rest of grammar. Sarah, the title of this episode is "Shorter Is Usually Right." Is that true?
SARAH: Usually. On ACT English, when two choices say the same thing and both are correct, the shorter one tends to win. Extra words that repeat an idea are wrong on this test. But notice the word usually. Shorter loses when it breaks a rule. So today is two rules, and then the shortcut.
SARAH: Rule one. Strip to the core. Find the subject and the verb, and cross out everything between them. Listen. "The box of old records are on the shelf." Sounds kind of okay, right?
KADEN: Kind of.
SARAH: Cross out "of old records." Now it's "The box are on the shelf."
KADEN: Oh, that's bad. The box is.
SARAH: The box is. The verb matches the box, not the records. The app warns you about this exact trap: matching the verb to a nearby noun instead of the real subject. The test often puts a plural noun right next to the verb to pull you in.
SARAH: Same idea for pronouns. A pronoun has to match the noun it stands for in number. "The team lost its first game." The team is one team, so on the ACT it's its, not their.
SARAH: And tense. Keep the verbs in the same time as the rest of the passage. If the paragraph is in the past, a random present-tense verb is probably the wrong answer.
LEHUA: Rule two. Modifiers.
SARAH: A modifier is a describing phrase. The rule is that it has to sit right next to the thing it describes. When that thing is in the wrong spot, it's a misplaced modifier. When it's missing from the sentence completely, it's a dangling modifier. Either way, the sentence says something ridiculous.
LEHUA: Let's do one. Read it twice.

SARAH: Again. "Driving up the hill, the fog made the road hard to see." A, No Change. B, "Driving up the hill, we could barely see the road through the fog." C, "While driving up the hill, the fog made the road hard to see." D, "Driving up the hill, the road was hard to see in the fog."

SARAH: The title says usually. Shorter wins only among the choices that are correct. Grammar first, then length. Rule, then shortcut.
DEREK: That's the same as math. The shortcut only counts if the answer is right.
LEHUA: Okay, now the shortcut itself.
SARAH: Redundancy. Saying the same thing twice. "In my opinion, I think the plan is good." "In my opinion" and "I think" are the same idea. Cut one. "The hotel was built recently, a few months ago." Recently and a few months ago, same idea. Cut one.
SARAH: And sometimes one of the choices is OMIT. That means delete the underlined part completely. When the sentence still makes sense and keeps its meaning without those words, OMIT is often right. So the habit is to check OMIT or the shortest choice first. If it keeps the meaning and it's grammatical, take it.
KADEN: So OMIT is like the skip button.
SARAH: OMIT is the skip button, but only when you lose nothing by skipping.
DEREK: And don't pick it because it's short. Read the sentence without the words. If it still says the same thing, good. If the meaning changes, it's wrong.
