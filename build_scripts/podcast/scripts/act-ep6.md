LEHUA: Good. Last week was rules. This week, there's no rule to memorize. Sarah, what are these questions?
SARAH: These are the questions where the ACT asks you to think like the writer. The question isn't "is this grammatical?" It's "what is this sentence for?" There are a few kinds. Some ask which choice best accomplishes a goal. Some ask whether the writer should add a sentence or delete it. Some ask which transition fits, and sometimes they ask where a sentence should go.
KADEN: Those are the ones where every answer is grammatically fine and I just pick a vibe.
SARAH: Exactly. And that's why people miss them. Every choice is a fine sentence. The question is which one does the job. So the first move is to read the question and find the job. The goal is written right there in the question. Most people skip past it to get to the choices.
LEHUA: Let's do one now, so we can talk about it.
SARAH: Here's the setup. A paragraph about a canoe club. Then the question. "The writer wants to show that the club welcomes beginners. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?" A, "The club was started more than forty years ago." B, "New paddlers can join any practice, and coaches pair them with experienced paddlers." C, "The club's canoes are heavy and hard to carry." D, "The club races most weekends in the summer."
SARAH: One more time. The goal is, show that the club welcomes beginners. A, "The club was started more than forty years ago." B, "New paddlers can join any practice, and coaches pair them with experienced paddlers." C, "The club's canoes are heavy and hard to carry." D, "The club races most weekends in the summer."
LEHUA: Pause it and try. Say the goal back first.
LEHUA: Okay, we're back. Kaden.
KADEN: The goal is beginners are welcome. So B. New paddlers, and coaches help them.
SARAH: B. Now let's look at the wrong ones, because that's where the lesson is. C. Heavy canoes. Does that show beginners are welcome?
KADEN: It kind of does the opposite. It sounds hard.
SARAH: Cross it out. D. Races most summer weekends. Maybe true, but nothing about beginners. Cross it out. And A. Started forty years ago. Same problem as D, and it's the trap we're naming today. The app calls it true but irrelevant.
KADEN: But it could be true.
SARAH: It could be totally true. It could be in the paragraph already. It might even be interesting. But the question asked for one job, welcoming beginners, and A doesn't do that job. True but irrelevant is one of the most tempting wrong answers on these questions, because it's real information. It just answers a question nobody asked.
DEREK: In math terms, it's a correct number for the wrong question.
SARAH: That's a good way to say it.
LEHUA: Okay, add or delete. Sarah.
SARAH: The question says something like, "The writer is considering deleting this sentence. Should the writer make this deletion?" And the choices are two yeses and two nos, each with a reason. Here's the test the app uses. Would the paragraph lose anything if this sentence vanished? If it would lose something it needs, keep it. If nothing is lost, delete it.
SARAH: And here's the part people miss. Getting yes or no right is only half the question. You still have to pick the right reason. The app warns you about exactly this trap: choosing yes or no correctly but picking the wrong reason. So decide yes or no, then read both reasons on your side and pick the one that matches what the sentence actually does.
KADEN: So it's like getting the right answer on a math problem but showing the wrong work.
SARAH: On the ACT, the wrong work costs you the point.
LEHUA: Last piece. Transitions.
SARAH: Read the sentence before the transition and the sentence after. Then name the link in one word. Contrast, like however. Cause, like therefore. Addition, like also. Or order, like then. The right transition has to fit both neighbors, not just the one after it.
SARAH: Quick one. "The trail was steep. Blank, the view at the top was worth it." What's the link?
KADEN: Steep is bad, view is good. So contrast. However.
SARAH: However. Not therefore. The steep trail didn't cause the good view.
DEREK: And if a question asks where a sentence should go, look for a word that points back. "This," "that," "these," "also." It has to come after the thing it's pointing at.
KADEN: So if a sentence starts with "these problems," the problems have to show up before it.
DEREK: Right. You can't point at something nobody has mentioned yet.
LEHUA: How long should these take, Sarah? They feel slower than grammar.
SARAH: They are a little slower. That's okay. You banked time on the grammar ones. The app's suggested time for these is about fifty seconds. Just don't reread the whole passage. Reread the paragraph around the sentence. That's enough.

For add or delete, ask if the paragraph loses anything, then pick yes or no and also the right reason. For transitions, name the link and make it fit both sentences.
