LEHUA: Hey, welcome back to SAT Notes, episode six. I'm Lehua. Sarah is here, Derek is here, and Kaden is here, and he says he only slept a little.
KADEN: I was in a ranked match. It went to overtime. We lost. I don't want to talk about it.
SARAH: We'll talk about it a little.
LEHUA: Callback first. Kaden, last episode. What's the move, and what's the trap?
KADEN: Predict, then match. Make my own answer before I look at the choices. The trap was too extreme. Right direction, but louder than the passage. Like indestructible when it said little damage.
SARAH: Perfect. Hold on to "louder than the passage," because today is the cousin of that trap.
LEHUA: Today is inferences and evidence. Sarah, go.
SARAH: Inference questions on the SAT usually end with a blank and ask, "Which choice most logically completes the text?" The passage builds an argument, and you supply the last step. Here's the rule the whole episode hangs on. The right answer is the smallest step. Not the most interesting step. The smallest one.
KADEN: Why would they want the boring one?
SARAH: Because the boring one is the one the passage actually supports. The test is checking whether you can stay inside the text. Every interesting answer brings in something the passage never said.
SARAH: So here are the steps. Find the last line before the blank and ask what it sets up. Predict what has to come next. Then pick the choice that needs the fewest new ideas. Anything the text never gave you, cut.
LEHUA: Okay, let's try one. Sarah, read it twice.
SARAH: Here it is. "A town added new streetlights to Main Street but not to Oak Street. The next year, nighttime accidents dropped on Main Street and stayed about the same on Oak Street. This suggests that blank."
SARAH: Again. "A town added new streetlights to Main Street but not to Oak Street. The next year, nighttime accidents dropped on Main Street and stayed about the same on Oak Street. This suggests that blank."
SARAH: Predict first. Then here are the choices. A, the new streetlights may have helped reduce nighttime accidents on Main Street. B, drivers on Oak Street are more careless than drivers on Main Street. C, streetlights will end nighttime accidents in every town. D, the town will add streetlights to Oak Street next.
SARAH: One more time. A, the streetlights may have helped on Main Street. B, Oak Street drivers are more careless. C, streetlights will end accidents in every town. D, the town will add lights to Oak Street next.
LEHUA: Pause it and try. Find the smallest step.
LEHUA: And we're back. Kaden?
KADEN: My prediction was, the lights helped. But D sounds smart. If the lights worked, obviously they put them on Oak Street next.
SARAH: Great, because that's exactly the trap. Does the passage say anything about the town's plans?
KADEN: No. It just says what happened.
SARAH: Right. To pick D, you have to bring in a new idea, that the town is planning more lights. The app has a name for that. Adds an assumption. The choice needs one extra idea the passage never gave you.
DEREK: B does the same thing. Nothing in the passage is about how careful the drivers are. You'd have to invent that.
SARAH: And C should sound familiar.
KADEN: Every town. That's too extreme. Last episode's trap.
SARAH: Exactly. One street in one town does not prove anything about every town. So we're left with A. And look at the word "may." The streetlights may have helped. That's soft, and soft is what the evidence earns. One street got lights, accidents dropped there, and the other street stayed the same. The smallest step is, the lights might be part of why. The answer is A.
KADEN: So my prediction was right, and then I talked myself out of it.
SARAH: That happens a lot. The trap choice is usually the one that feels clever. When a choice makes you think, "Oh, and then they would," ask yourself where the passage said that.
LEHUA: Okay, the evidence questions. Those are different, right?
SARAH: They're flipped. Inference gives you the evidence and asks for the claim. Command of Evidence gives you the claim and asks for the evidence. The steps are, find the exact claim the question wants supported. Say it in your own words. Then test each choice. Does this prove this claim directly? A true statement that doesn't support the claim is still wrong.
LEHUA: And some of those have a chart.
DEREK: That's mine. Some evidence questions give you a table or a graph, and the choices quote numbers from it. Three checks. Read the labels, read the units, then check the exact numbers against the claim.
DEREK: Quick one, no pause. Say the claim is, "Nighttime accidents on Main Street fell by more than half." A choice says accidents went from forty to twenty-two. Does that support the claim?
KADEN: It went down a lot, so yes?
DEREK: Half of forty is twenty. Twenty-two is more than twenty. So it fell by less than half. The number is real, the chart is real, and the choice still doesn't support this claim. You have to do the arithmetic.
LEHUA: So the number is true. It just doesn't prove the point.
DEREK: Right. True, and still the wrong answer.
SARAH: And on the chart questions, the passage still matters. The claim comes from the text. The chart is just the proof. Read the claim first, then go to the chart looking for one thing.
LEHUA: Sarah, real question. Did you read anything this week with a bad inference in it?
SARAH: My mom texted, "Your brother didn't call, so he must be mad at me." That is a huge assumption. Also she put a comma before "so," which is correct, and I was so proud I forgot to answer the actual question.
LEHUA: Was he mad?
SARAH: His phone was dead.
LEHUA: Recap time. Kaden.
KADEN: On "most logically completes," the right answer is the smallest step, not the most exciting one. Find the last line, predict, pick the choice that needs the fewest new ideas. The trap is adds an assumption, like saying the town will put lights on Oak Street next when the passage never said that. Watch for soft words like may, because soft is what the evidence earns. For evidence questions, it's the other way. Say the claim in my own words, then find the choice that proves that exact claim. On charts, check the labels, the units, and do the math.
DEREK: You got the chart part, and you admitted you talked yourself out of the right answer. Eight.
KADEN: I'll take eight.
