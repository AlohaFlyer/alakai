# SAT Notes Ep 7: Where the Sentence Breaks

facts: Boundaries is an SAT Standard English Conventions skill covering punctuation between and within clauses (College Board skill list, satsuite.collegeboard.org/sat/whats-on-the-test/reading-writing; data/sat/skills.json id rw-sec-boundaries); standard punctuation rules: two independent clauses are joined by a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet); a comma alone between two independent clauses is a comma splice; a semicolon needs an independent clause on both sides; a colon follows a complete independent clause and introduces an explanation, example, or list; a pair of dashes sets off extra information the way a pair of commas does; a dependent clause that opens a sentence is followed by a comma (College Board Standard English Conventions; Purdue OWL punctuation guides); label-both-sides steps and the "comma splice because it reads naturally aloud" trap from data/strategies.json rw-sec-boundaries; app trap names "near miss" and "adds an assumption" from assets/engine.js; worked example is an original item written for the show; paddling practice at the harbor (cast.json places, outrigger clubs practice out of the harbor, no times stated)

SARAH: Boundaries questions ask what punctuation goes between two parts of a sentence. And here's the good news. You don't need to know every rule in the grammar book. You need one move. Label both sides.
SARAH: Find where the punctuation goes. Then look at the words on the left and ask, could this stand alone as a complete sentence? Then do the same on the right. Each side is either independent, which means it's a complete sentence, or dependent, which means it isn't.
KADEN: How do I know if it's complete?
SARAH: It needs a subject and a verb, and it has to make sense by itself. "She still had to print it" is complete. "Because she still had to print it" is not. That word "because" makes it lean on something else.
DEREK: It's like checking both sides of an equation before you touch anything.
SARAH: Exactly. Once both sides are labeled, the punctuation picks itself. If both sides are independent, you have three choices. A period. A semicolon. Or a comma plus a joining word like and, but, or so. What you can't use is a comma alone.
LEHUA: And that's called?
SARAH: A comma splice. Two complete sentences glued together with just a comma. It's the most common wrong answer in this whole skill.

DEREK: So "it sounds fine" is not a test.
SARAH: Not on Boundaries. Your ear is great for a lot of grammar. It's bad at telling a comma from a period. Now, C, no punctuation, runs the two sentences together with nothing. That's wrong too. D puts a comma between the subject, she, and the verb, had. You never split a subject from its verb with one comma. So the answer is B, the semicolon. Two complete sides, and a semicolon is one of the three legal ways to join them.
KADEN: What if there were a choice with a comma and "but"?
SARAH: Then that would also be correct, which is why you won't see both in the same question. Each question has only one legal option.
LEHUA: Okay, colons and dashes. People are scared of those.
SARAH: Two quick rules. A colon comes after a complete sentence, and it introduces something. An explanation, an example, or a list. "She had one job left: printing the essay." The left side has to be complete. The right side doesn't.
SARAH: Dashes come in pairs when they set off extra information, like a pair of commas. "Maya, my cousin, finished at midnight." You could write that with two dashes instead of two commas. What you can't do is open with a dash and close with a comma. Pick one and match it.
DEREK: Matching pairs. That one I like.
SARAH: And one more. If the sentence opens with a dependent clause, like "Because she finished late," you put a comma after it, then the main clause. "Because she finished late, she printed it in the morning."
LEHUA: So the semicolon rule is?
SARAH: A semicolon needs a complete sentence on both sides. Every time. If one side is dependent, a semicolon is wrong.
