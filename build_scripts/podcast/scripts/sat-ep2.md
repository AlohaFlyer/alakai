# SAT Notes Ep 2: Crossing Out: How to Find the Wrong Answers
cast: lehua, sarah, derek, kaden

LEHUA: Hey, welcome back to SAT Notes, episode two. I'm Lehua. Sarah is here, Derek is here, and Kaden is here with a hoodie over his whole face.
KADEN: It's cold in here. I'm conserving heat.
SARAH: It's a recording booth, not a freezer.
LEHUA: Okay, Kaden, warm-up question from last time. What's the one rule about blanks on the SAT?
KADEN: No blanks. A wrong answer and a blank both get zero, so a blank can never help you. Answer everything.
DEREK: Correct. No guessing penalty.
LEHUA: Good. So if you have to answer everything, the next question is obvious. How do you answer well when you don't know? That's today. Crossing out.
SARAH: And I want to say this right away. Crossing out is not a backup plan for when you're lost. It's how good test takers find the right answer on purpose. You don't hunt for the right answer first. You hunt for the wrong ones, because wrong answers have fingerprints.
KADEN: Fingerprints?
SARAH: Patterns. The test writers build wrong answers on purpose, and they build them the same few ways every time. Once you know the patterns, you start seeing them everywhere.
DEREK: Let me do the math part first, because it's short. Every SAT multiple-choice question has four choices. A blind guess is one in four. Cross out one choice you can prove is wrong, and you're at one in three. Cross out two, and it's a coin flip. Cross out three, and you didn't guess at all. You solved it backward.
KADEN: Okay, that's actually kind of fire. Like a boss fight where you take out the minions first and the boss is just standing there alone.
LEHUA: There it is.
DEREK: And Bluebook has a tool for this built in. You can strike out choices right on the screen. The app has the same thing, a little x next to every choice.
SARAH: One rule with the x. Only cross out a choice when you can say why it's wrong. Out loud in your head, one sentence. If you can't say why, leave it standing.
KADEN: What happens if I cross out the right one by accident?
SARAH: The app will tell you. If you miss a question and you had crossed out the right answer, it says so. That's a sign you were tapping the x on a feeling, not a reason.
LEHUA: So, Sarah, the fingerprints. What are they?
SARAH: The app gives every wrong answer a name when you miss it. It shows up as a little label, like "Trap: opposite." There are ten names in the app. Today we'll use the six you meet most in Reading and Writing, because those are the ones you can spot while you read.
SARAH: One. Too extreme. The choice says always, never, completely, or proves, when the passage only says some or might.
SARAH: Two. Half right. The first half of the choice matches the passage, and the second half goes wrong.
SARAH: Three. True but irrelevant. The choice is a real detail from the passage, but it doesn't answer this question.
SARAH: Four. Opposite. The choice flips what the passage says. It uses the right topic, but points the wrong direction.
SARAH: Five. Adds an assumption. The choice needs one extra idea the passage never gave you.
SARAH: Six. Near miss. Mostly grammar. It looks almost right but has one small error, like a comma in the wrong place.
KADEN: That's a lot of names.
LEHUA: You don't have to memorize them today. When you miss one in the app, read the label. After a week, you'll know them the way you know your friends' ringtones.
SARAH: Let's do one. This is a Words in Context question. When I say "blank," that's the missing word. I'll read it twice. Kaden, just listen.
SARAH: "The coach was usually blunt, but after the loss she was unusually blank, choosing every word carefully so no player felt singled out." Which word fits the blank? A, blunt. B, diplomatic. C, timid. D, talkative.
SARAH: One more time. "The coach was usually blunt, but after the loss she was unusually blank, choosing every word carefully so no player felt singled out." A, blunt. B, diplomatic. C, timid. D, talkative.
LEHUA: Everybody listening, pause it and try. Cross out what you can, and say why.
LEHUA: Okay, we're back. Kaden, go.
KADEN: I want to say timid. Because she's being careful.
SARAH: Good, you're thinking, so let's walk it the slow way. Start with the clues. The sentence says usually blunt, but. That word "but" means the blank is going to be different from blunt. So which choice can we cross out first?
KADEN: A. Blunt. It's literally the word she's not being.
SARAH: Right. And that's the trap we're naming today. Opposite. The sentence flips away from blunt, and choice A flips it right back. Opposite answers love to borrow a word straight from the passage, because it feels familiar.
SARAH: Next clue. "Choosing every word carefully so no player felt singled out." That's about how she talks to people. Which choice has nothing to do with that?
KADEN: D. Talkative. Nothing says she talked a lot.
SARAH: Cross it out. Now it's timid against diplomatic. And this is where it gets good. Timid has the right idea. She is being careful. But timid means scared or shy. Does the sentence say she's scared?
KADEN: No. It says she's careful so nobody feels bad.
SARAH: So timid is right idea, wrong word. It's close in feeling but wrong in meaning. Diplomatic means careful with words so you don't hurt people. That matches every clue. The answer is B.
KADEN: Okay, so I was close.
DEREK: Close is how they get you. The test writers want you to stop at the first choice that feels okay.
SARAH: Here's the habit. Before you pick, cross out until you have a reason for every x. If you get down to two and they both feel fine, put each one into the sentence and read it again. The wrong one usually sounds off in one specific spot.
LEHUA: And what if you get down to two and still can't decide?
DEREK: Then you pick one and move on. You have a fifty-fifty shot, which is twice as good as a blind guess. Flag it if you want to come back. Don't burn two minutes on it.
LEHUA: My auntie posted "let's eat grandma" on her page last week. No comma. I screenshotted it for you, Sarah.
SARAH: I saw it. I commented with the comma. She did not ask.
KADEN: Did she reply?
SARAH: She deleted my comment. Worth it.
LEHUA: Okay, bring it home. Kaden, recap. In your own words.
KADEN: Okay. Don't hunt for the right answer first. Hunt for the wrong ones. Every cross-out makes a guess better, four to three to two. Only cross out when I can say why it's wrong. The app names the traps, like too extreme, half right, true but irrelevant, opposite, adds an assumption, and near miss. Today's trap was opposite, where the choice flips what the passage says, and it usually steals a word from the passage. And if I'm down to two, plug both in, pick one, and move on.
