# SAT Notes Ep 1: How This Test Actually Works
cast: lehua, derek, pono, kaden
topic: Structure, adaptive modules, scores, no blanks
facts: Digital SAT taken in Bluebook; Reading and Writing 2 modules x 27 questions x 32 min; Math 2 modules x 22 questions x 35 min; total 2 hr 14 min plus one 10-minute break between sections; adaptive by module (module 1 mixed difficulty, module 2 easier or harder); a few unscored pretest questions per module; section scores 200 to 800, total 400 to 1600; no guessing penalty; Math about 75% multiple choice, 25% student-produced response; Desmos graphing calculator built in; class of 2025 national mean total 1029 (College Board 2025 Total Group Report); the paper SAT it replaced in 2024 was 3 hours without the essay (College Board), so the digital test is shorter; UH Manoa (flagship) is test-optional with a "Do No Harm" policy that excludes a submitted score that would harm admission or scholarship chances, and scholarships or department direct entry programs may require scores (manoa.hawaii.edu/admissions/freshman, checked 2026-10-16); many public universities are test-optional and may use scores for merit scholarships (school admissions pages); most colleges publish a middle 50 percent score range for admitted or enrolled students; several highly selective private colleges reinstated required testing for recent admission cycles (school admissions pages); students may bring an approved calculator, and a reference sheet of math formulas is provided (College Board calculator policy); Reading and Writing is one short passage per question; Bluebook has Mark for Review, an answer eliminator, and a question navigation grid (College Board Bluebook testing tools)
target_minutes: 8

DEREK: That was true a long time ago. It is not true now. Let me lay it out.
DEREK: The SAT is digital. You take it on a laptop or tablet in an app called Bluebook. It has two sections. Reading and Writing comes first, then Math.
DEREK: Each section is split into two modules. A Reading and Writing module is twenty-seven questions in thirty-two minutes. A Math module is twenty-two questions in thirty-five minutes. So the whole test is two hours and fourteen minutes of testing, with one ten-minute break between the two sections.
KADEN: Wait, that's it? That's like one long movie with all the trailers.
LEHUA: About that, yeah. Way shorter than the old paper test. And most people don't know that.
PONO: Okay, now the part that makes everybody nervous. The test is adaptive. Listen, dis one is important.
PONO: Module one is a mix of easy, medium, and hard questions. Everybody gets a module like that. How you do on module one decides your module two. Do well, and module two is the harder version. Do less well, and module two is the easier version.
KADEN: So if I get the hard one, that means I'm doing bad?
PONO: The opposite. The harder module two is the one that can give you the higher score. If module two feels tough, that is usually good news.
PONO: It's like a rodeo draw. Ride your first horse clean, and they give you the horse that scores big points. The rough horse is the prize.
KADEN: I don't want the rough horse.
PONO: You want the rough horse. Trust me.
LEHUA: And this is why we keep saying module one matters. Each module has its own clock, so rushing module one doesn't save you any time for later. It just costs you accuracy.
DEREK: One more detail. A few questions in each module are unscored. College Board is testing them for future tests. You can't tell which ones they are, so treat every question as if it counts.
KADEN: Okay, scoring. What's a good score? Like, what's the number.
DEREK: Each section is scored from two hundred to eight hundred. Add them together and the total runs from four hundred to sixteen hundred.
DEREK: For context, the national average total for the class of twenty twenty-five was one thousand twenty-nine. That's from College Board's own report.
KADEN: So anything over that is good?
DEREK: Anything over that is above average. Whether it's good depends on where you're applying.
LEHUA: Right, and this is the part I want Big Island kids to hear. A good score is not one number. It's a number compared to the schools on your list.
DEREK: Most colleges publish a middle fifty percent range for their admitted or enrolled students. That means a quarter of those students scored below the low number, and a quarter scored above the high number. You can find it on the school's admissions page, or on College Board's BigFuture site.
DEREK: So the rule is this. If your score is inside that range, you're a normal applicant there. If it's at or above the top of the range, your score helps you. If it's below the bottom, think hard about whether to send it.
KADEN: What about UH? Most of my friends are going to UH.
DEREK: Good question. As of this fall, the flagship UH campus is test-optional. You choose whether to send a score. And they have what they call a "Do No Harm" policy. If a score you send would hurt your chances for admission or a scholarship, they leave it out.
KADEN: Wait, so there's no downside to sending it?
DEREK: For admission there, by their own policy, no. And some scholarships and some direct-entry programs may want a score, so a good score can still mean money. Check the admissions page for your year, because policies change.
LEHUA: What about mainland schools? Mainland public universities and private colleges.
DEREK: A lot of mainland public universities are test-optional too, and a strong score can still help with merit scholarships. Several highly selective private colleges have gone back to requiring scores in recent years. So the honest answer is, look up every school on your list, every year. Don't trust what your cousin heard two years ago.
